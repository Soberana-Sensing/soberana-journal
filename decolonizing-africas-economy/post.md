Africa is the most resource‑rich continent on the planet holding the [greatest variety and concentration of precious metals and minerals, largest area of arable land](https://www.unep.org/regions/africa/our-work-africa), and the [fastest‑growing working population](https://cap-a.org/home/the-promise-of-africa/assets/youngest-and-fastest-growing-workforce/) on Earth. Despite those advantages, African economies are still based around the extraction of unprocessed raw materials meant for export following practices of the colonial era. To this day, Africa [exports cheap raw commodities and imports higher-value manufactured products](https://www.africanexponent.com/why-africa-poor-despite-rich-resources/) leading to [a cycle of net deficit](https://unctadstat.unctad.org/insights/theme/228) that keeps countries "economically hostage" to a global financial architecture dominated by Western powers and the US dollar.

It is important to note that [the "Global North" would not have enjoyed wealth and development of such magnitude without the exploitation of Africa](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S095937802200005X) through the unequal exchange of labour in the world economy. [Hickel et al. (2024)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-49687-y.pdf) found that workers in the "Global South", who receive an average €1.62 per hour, perform:

- **90%** of all the labour (in hours) that goes into making everything the world consumes and invests in,
- **91%** of the labour that produces traded goods, and
- **46%** of the labour that supports growth and consumption in the "Global North" (net of trade).

Figure 1 shows that the **wage value of labour net‑appropriated from the "Global South" increased by 42% between 1995 and 2021**. Yet Southern workers, who contribute the majority of labour that powers the world economy, receive only 21% of global GDP. The yields of production are disproportionately captured in the "Global North". It, therefore follows that the global economy is supported by a **regime of cheap labour**.

![Bar chart showing the wage value of labour net-appropriated from the global South, 1995 to 2021, shaded from lower to higher skill level.](images/labour-value-appropriation.webp "Figure 1. **The wage value of labour net-appropriated from the global South in trillion Euros (constant 2021), by skill level, 1995–2021.** Skill levels are shaded from lighter (low-skilled) to darker (high-skilled).")

If Africa (and more generally, the "Global South") was economically sovereign—controlling its own food, energy, and industrial systems—[the imperialist states would lose access to ultra‑cheap materials and labour that underpin their prosperity](https://pure.coventry.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/94047054/LDPI2_WPS_2024-039_Tilzey.pdf) and allow for [investments in military equipment and technology](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/sp/gxeu01-defence-technology-investment/) used to continue the cycle of oppression and exploitation of people around the world. In other words, imperialism¹ would become **economically infeasible.** Furthermore, recent [analysis by Prof. Ruolz Ariste](https://theconversation.com/guns-over-people-rising-military-spending-is-eroding-quality-of-life-around-the-world-279601) shows that surging military budgets are not neutral “security” spending: they systematically crowd out investment in health, education, housing, and green infrastructure, lowering living standards for the majority while enriching arms producers and shareholders. Far from making societies safer, this reallocation of public resources toward weapons **weakens social welfare and deepens inequality**, making high defense spending a net negative for society as a whole.

In that sense, **Africa’s liberation is a precondition for global liberation**.

# The "Aid Trap" and the case for "dedollarisation"

The majority of [African countries spent more repaying external debts than on healthcare or education](https://debtjustice.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Between-Life-and-Debt.pdf)—and in some cases both—in 2023. This pattern is widely interpreted in critical scholarship as a symptom of neocolonial structures that reproduce "Global North" dominance by locking African states into debt‑servicing and austerity, thereby constraining their policy sovereignty and development options.

The structural character of this burden was already powerfully articulated by Thomas Sankara, then president of Burkina Faso, in his 1987 [speech to the Organisation of African Unity in Addis Ababa](https://www.marxists.org/archive/sankara/1987/july/29.htm). He declared: _“Debt’s origins come from colonialism’s origins. Those who lend us money are those who colonized us. They are the same ones who used to manage our states and economies. These are the colonizers who indebted Africa through their brothers and cousins, who were the lenders. We had no connections with this debt. Therefore we cannot pay for it. […] Under its current form, controlled and dominated by imperialism, debt is a skillfully managed reconquest of Africa, intended to subjugate its growth and development through foreign rules. Thus, each one of us becomes the financial slave, which is to say a true slave, of those who had been treacherous enough to put money in our countries with obligations for us to repay.”_ Sankara went on to call for a collective refusal of repayment, arguing that _“Debt cannot be repaid, first because if we don’t repay, lenders will not die. That is for sure. But if we repay, we are going to die.”_

Sankara, essentially, argued that African peoples and states had not contracted these obligations as equal partners, but inherited a debt regime that is itself an instrument of domination. Therefore, the present populations and governments of African states are not morally or politically liable for debts imposed under conditions of domination and asymmetry.

The debt relations Sankara denounced have persisted within the same dollar‑centered international monetary order that already structured African borrowing in the 1980s. Because most [external debt, trade invoicing, and essential imports are denominated in US dollars, African states must constantly acquire dollars to service debt and purchase food, fuel, and manufactured goods](https://globalsouthperspectives.substack.com/p/a-comprehensive-and-coherent-global). This dependence gives the United States and allied financial institutions leverage to impose sanctions, conditionality, and policy prescriptions that reproduce the very external deficits and austerity that generate new rounds of borrowing. In this sense, **dollar hegemony functions as the monetary infrastructure of neocolonial debt**.

## The cycle that needs breaking

As discussed, countries today still depend on US dollar reserves for their imports. This gives the US the power to sanction and effectively intimidate countries into accepting unfair agreement conditions. [Structural trade deficits such as the case of Africa initiate a detrimental cycle](https://www.patreon.com/upstreampodcast/posts/rise-and-fall-of-159401405):

1. Chronic trade deficits push down the value of local currencies against the dollar, creating weak exchange rates.
2. A weaker currency makes imports of vital products (food, fuel, medicine, etc.) more expensive, effectively “importing inflation” into the most sensitive parts of the economy.
3. Rising prices for essentials create social unrest, leading governments to subsidize these sectors to protect vulnerable groups.
4. These subsidies are unsustainable; to stabilize or artificially strengthen the exchange rate, governments borrow more US dollars, accumulating external debt.
5. The US and international financial institutions then push for activities that **prioritize dollar‑generation** (mining, raw mineral exports, cash crops).
6. This cycle undermines **food security** and **industrialization**, feeding back into the original trade deficits, keeping the African continent in debt **by design**.

**Dedollarization, therefore, should stand at the core of every national and decolonial movement.** To break this cycle regional cooperation and Pan-African planning are essential. As economist Fadhel Kaboub argues, dedollarization cannot be declared into existence by a presidential decree, nor does it require a new currency. Dedollarization can be achieved via strategic investments that enhance economic and monetary sovereignty at the national or regional level. It rests on a three‑legged stool: **food sovereignty, energy sovereignty, and industrial/technological sovereignty.** Therefore, the main sources of external borrowing that must be addressed first to achieve true sovereignty are: food imports, energy imports and imports of high-value-added manufactured and technological products.

![footnote] ¹Imperialism as defined in [modern frameworks](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21598282.2015.1031943) where it is understood as the intertwined political and economic project through which the imperial state and its allied institutions secure and expand the conditions for global capital accumulation. Economically, it operates through investments, production, trade, and market expansion to penetrate existing markets, open new ones, exploit labour, extract surplus value, and secure raw materials. Politically, it is carried out by the imperial state and its agencies—including international financial and security institutions such as the World Bank—which shape the institutional, regulatory, and geopolitical environment to “pave the way” for capital. Social inequalities, unemployment, poverty, and environmental degradation are not goals of this project but are treated as structural, often unavoidable side‑effects of development that must be managed in the interests of security and continued accumulation.
